    Timelines and diagnostics¶

    Before aggregating a benchmark, look at one problem. A timeline shows where the invocation waited, what ran early, and what survived; a wall-clock number alone cannot tell you whether the overlap you expected happened.

    Render a timeline¶

    from agentir import render_timeline
from agentir.diagnostics.viz import TimelinePanel

rows = runtime.ledger_for("first")
events = runtime.events_for("first")
render_timeline(TimelinePanel("spec on, W=2", rows, events), "first.svg", title="first program")

    The SVG is written by hand, with no plotting dependency. Pass a list of panels to stack runs on one time axis; agentir check --runtime-graph does exactly that for the spec-off and spec-on arms, and agentir bench --runtime-graph DIR writes one file per problem and arm. --dump-ledger DIR saves the raw rows as JSONL.

    Read the chart¶

    Each engine node is a lane. A diamond marks registration; a thin gray line is the queue between data-ready and issue; the bar runs from issue to done, with a lighter head for prefill and a shaded body for decode.

    Color Meaning
    blue issued while the guard was pending (speculative)
    green committed; a blue bar turns green at guard resolution
    red wasted or failed: finished or aborted in a losing world
    amber band control-plane ops, packed into lanes
    dashed red vertical a decision resolved

    Registered-but-never-issued nodes appear as markers only, with "squashed before issue" when their guard turned false first. The header of each panel summarizes wall time, committed and squashed counts, speculative hit rate, accumulated lead, and committed versus wasted tokens.

    The ledger fields¶

    Field Meaning A long gap before it means
    t_reg the tracer registered the node the program had not reached this call yet
    t_data_ready every referenced output existed an upstream generation, op, or tool was still running
    t_issue a physical attempt started no free slot, budget exhausted, or lower funding priority
    t_first first stream event prefill and engine queueing; equals t_done for final-granularity attempts
    t_done the stream ended decode time
    t_guard_resolved the keep-or-cancel decision landed selection latency

    speculative says how the node was issued; disposition says what happened to its work. Both matter: a node can be issued speculatively and commit, or finish committed-priority and still be wasted later. cached_tokens is the engine's own prefix-cache count; -1 means the engine did not report it. fork_parent marks nodes whose prompt extends a parent's full state, which is what makes a cache hit possible, not proof of one.

    Diagnose a disappointing run¶

    What you see Look at
    no blue bars did the decision resolve before its children were data-ready? A cheap vote hides nothing. Check window and max_spec_inflight
    long gray queues on green bars the engine is full; committed work is waiting behind other committed work, or max_inflight is low
    mostly red wide pools with poor selection accuracy, or a deep window over an unlikely branch
    large lead sum, small wall gain leads overlap in time, or something else is on the critical path (a PRM sweep waiting for text, a render round trip)
    correct predictions, no gain the tool is fast, the predictor is slow, or remote publishes calls only at turn end

    The lead metric sums t_guard_resolved − t_issue over committed speculative nodes. Several nodes accumulate lead over the same interval, so it is an upper bound on saved time, never an elapsed-time figure.

    Beyond the ledger¶

    runtime.snapshot() returns scheduler counters (pending, in flight, spec in flight, engine recovery state) and tool-scheduler queue depths. runtime.counters holds named event counts: preempted, pruned, spec_retries, prefetch_issued, engine_deaths, and others. bench.gpu_telemetry and bench.vllm_metrics_telemetry sample nvidia-smi and vLLM's Prometheus endpoint into JSONL for experiments; their idle-time statistics are proxies and are named as such.
